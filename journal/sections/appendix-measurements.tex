\section{Secondary measurements}\label{app:measurements}

Five measurements are reported here rather than in the body. Each
supports a headline the body states; none carries a conclusion of its
own.

\subsection{Latency}\label{app:latency}

\Cref{sec:cost} reports calls and tokens, both exact from the shared
meter. Wall-clock time tells the same story in seconds: a mean of $11.2$
and $17.2$ per question for AGR against $12.9$ and $15.2$ for the
baseline, and between $1.0$ and $2.2$ for the three single-call systems.
Read those as magnitudes only. Records replayed entirely from a response
cache are excluded from those means, but between $3.6\%$ and $15.2\%$ of
the two navigators' calls in the remaining records were still served
from the cache, which shortens a cached call to nothing. The figures are
therefore lower bounds and do not rank the two navigators against each
other at the second.

\subsection{Positioning against published RoG results}\label{sec:rog}

The environment of \Cref{sec:environment} is built from the per-question
subgraphs that the RoG distribution publishes, and
RoG~\cite{luo2024reasoning} is the closest published system in the
path-retrieval family, so a reader who notices both will ask how the two
compare. The aggregate answer does not favour this work, and we give it
before the qualifications. Over the full test splits RoG publishes
$85.7$ Hits@1 and $70.8$ F1 on WebQSP and $62.6$ and $56.2$ on
ComplexWebQuestions, against this work's $75.5$ and $64.2$, and $52.2$
and $46.9$ over the certified $400$-question samples of those same
splits. \textbf{RoG leads on all four figures, and the margin is not
sampling noise}: AGR's $95\%$ intervals on Hits@1 are $[71.3, 79.7]$ on
WebQSP and $[47.5, 57.0]$ on ComplexWebQuestions, and RoG's $85.7$ and
$62.6$ fall outside both.

One difference bounds what that comparison establishes, and it is
decisive. \textbf{RoG is trained on these benchmarks; AGR is trained on
nothing.} RoG fine-tunes LLaMA2-Chat-7B on the training splits of both
benchmarks, which hold $2{,}826$ and $27{,}639$ questions in the
distribution used here, while AGR's backbone is frozen and prompted and
has seen no question from either benchmark before evaluation. Three
ordinary differences follow it: the backbones differ, which is the very
factor \Cref{sec:baselines} holds constant elsewhere; RoG reports over
the full splits against our stratified samples of them and publishes no
interval, so the gap can be bounded from one side only; and RoG
retrieves within each question's own published subgraph while AGR
navigates the union of all of them. What the comparison settles is AGR's
place in the published landscape, a training-free system against a
supervised one on the same question sets. What it does not settle is
anything this paper measures, since neither the ablation of
\Cref{sec:attribution} nor the census of \Cref{sec:erroranalysis} has a
counterpart in RoG's evaluation.

\subsection{Accuracy by hop depth}\label{sec:hops}

Stratifying by the number of hops separating a topic entity from the
gold answer separates the systems further. On ComplexWebQuestions, AGR
is the only system whose accuracy \emph{rises} with hop count: $0.46$ at
one hop, $0.55$ at two, $0.57$ at three or more, over strata of $137$,
$211$, and $49$ questions. The last step sits well inside the interval
of a $49$-question stratum, so the claim is the direction across the
three strata, not the size of any one step. Every other system ends
below where it started. That phrasing is deliberate and the weaker
alternative is the true one: Think-on-Graph does not decay monotonically
but falls from $0.53$ to $0.37$ and then partly recovers to $0.45$. It
also beats AGR on that dataset's one-hop stratum, $0.53$ against $0.46$,
and \Cref{sec:planner} identifies the mechanism, since decomposition
costs accuracy on questions that do not need decomposing and that
stratum is full of them.

The static graph baseline's decay across these strata is deliberately
\emph{not} offered as evidence for the paradigm, because its retrieval
radius confounds it, and the evidence that it is a radius effect is in
the strata themselves. GraphRAG scores $0.44$ on WebQSP's two-hop
stratum, where those questions are largely mediator paths a one-hop
expansion does reach, against $0.16$ on the ComplexWebQuestions two-hop
stratum, where the chains are genuine compositions it cannot reach at
all. A retriever failing uniformly on depth would not show that gap. A
rising curve is what decomposition would predict, but it is consistent
with that mechanism rather than evidence for it, and the WebQSP panel
carries no comparable evidence: its three-hop stratum contains four
questions, which is why the claim is scoped to one dataset.

\subsection{The next experiments, and what each would
  settle}\label{app:experiments}

\Cref{sec:outlook} names four follow-ups in the order they would be run.
First, the agentic baseline at AGR's candidate widths under a sweep of
the call cap, with the budget split of \Cref{tab:togsplit} reported at
each setting, which tests whether the affordability reading survives
equal access to the graph. Second, replication: every system and every
ablation condition run several times, so that a delta of a few
hundredths of F1 can be read against the run-to-run variance rather than
assumed to exceed it, together with the joint-removal condition
\Cref{sec:power} names. Third, the full test splits, which quadruple the
discordant pairs the paired tests see. Fourth, one logging change:
persist each accepted claim with the route that accepted it and the
triples that matched, which makes the verifier's precision and recall a
number a labelled sample can produce.

\subsection{What bounds the two larger nulls}\label{app:nulldetail}

\Cref{sec:nulls} reports that backtracking and model scoring change
nothing detectable. Two implementation facts bound those nulls further,
and we record them rather than smooth them over.

The backtracker bans the edges of the most recent explorer pass but
restores the highest-scoring snapshot, which is often older, so
everything expanded in between stays unbanned (\ref{app:routing}). Over
the main runs, $53$ explorer passes re-expanded an anchor-and-relation
set the same question had already expanded, and at least $65$ of the
$262$ backtracks restored a depth other than the most recent snapshot's.
The null therefore describes backtracking as implemented, not
backtracking as specified.

The development-set sweep that froze $\alpha$ points the same way as the
scoring null at a smaller scale: at $\tau = 0.20$ it scored $61$, $62$,
$64$, and $59$ hits of $80$ at $\alpha = 0.3$, $0.5$, $0.7$, and $1.0$,
so dropping the model term cost five hits there, before the
entity-filter correction that preceded the test runs. One confound goes
with it: at $\alpha = 1$ the scorer takes an early return that leaves
the backtracking trigger's signal maximum computed over a smaller
candidate set, so that condition's elevated backtrack count is not
attributable to the scoring change alone.

\subsection{What an equal-width re-run would and would not
  settle}\label{app:equalwidth}

\Cref{sec:margin} reports that the agentic baseline searched a narrower
candidate pool than AGR, so its figures on the questions it finishes are
a lower bound rather than an estimate, and that equalising the widths is
the first change we would make in a follow-up. That re-run would not
vary one thing, though. Wider candidate sets make each pruning call
dearer, so equal-width pruning would also raise the baseline's clip
rate, moving the boundary between the two halves of \Cref{tab:togsplit}
at the same time as it changes what the search resolves within each. The
two effects have to be read apart, which means reporting that split
again at each setting rather than an aggregate in which they cancel or
compound invisibly.

A related check bounds the aggregate from the other side, because a
metric that scores a hit on any match rewards naming more entities. On
the WebQSP questions it answers, AGR names $3.37$ entities against
Think-on-Graph's $2.56$, and its entity precision over those same
answered questions is $0.760$ against $0.703$; on ComplexWebQuestions
the figures are $1.54$ against $1.40$ entities and $0.629$ against
$0.520$ precision. If AGR bought breadth without accuracy, precision
would fall as the entity count rose. It does not fall.

\subsection{Which systems joined each cleared
  consensus}\label{app:participation}

\Cref{sec:defects} reports that the systems agreeing on a cleared
consensus answer are not independent observers. These are the counts
behind that. On ComplexWebQuestions the no-retrieval control, which sees
no graph at all, is among the agreeing systems on $25$ of the $31$
cleared rows and on $11$ of the $16$ echo rows, so there the convergence
is at least as much a property of what the backbone believes as of the
graph's neighbourhood structure. On WebQSP the control joins $15$ of
$54$ rows and $13$ of $36$ echo rows, and the two navigators agree with
each other on $20$ of the $36$. Of the $13$ rows on which all five
systems agreed, $9$ were cleared as not a defect.

\subsection{The verifier's unmeasured exposure, in
  claims}\label{app:exposure}

\Cref{sec:limits-contribution} names wrongful acceptance as the most
serious gap in this work and says why the committed record cannot close
it. This is its scale in the claim unit. There is no way, from that
record, to know how many wrongful acceptances sit inside the $761$
questions that ended grounded. On test the verifier decomposed $2{,}110$
claims and accepted $2{,}008$ of them; the graph-wide adjacency probe
accounts for $39$ of those acceptances, and the rest were made either by
traversed adjacency, which is equally relation-blind, or by the
entailment check, which is not, and the record does not say which. So
the share of accepted claims certified without any test of the asserted
relation lies somewhere in $[39, 2{,}008]$. That interval is two orders
of magnitude wide, and we report it as one rather than choose a point
inside it. The same gap means the layer has no measured precision or
recall as a classifier of claims, which is the first number a
verification component should carry.

The output contract is bounded by the same logging decision. On test,
$12$ of AGR's $367$ answered WebQSP questions and $9$ of its $308$ on
ComplexWebQuestions were emitted with a count of zero supporting
triples, which is the contract's own measure of how often it delivers
nothing.

\subsection{Sensitivity of the tables to the
  exclusions}\label{app:sensitivity}

\Cref{sec:defects} reports that the accuracy tables are not rescored
against the adjudicated labels. This is how much that decision could
matter. The defects cut both ways: $10$ of the $22$ WebQSP exclusions
and $4$ of the $19$ on ComplexWebQuestions are questions AGR scored as
hits against a label now judged defective. Recomputing every cell of
\Cref{tab:main} with the adjudicated questions removed moves each system
by between $+0.001$ and $+0.020$ Hits@1 and leaves the ranking on both
datasets unchanged. That shift is an order of magnitude below the
$5.5\%$ and $4.8\%$ defect rates, because removing a question the system
got right subtracts from the numerator as well as the denominator. The
exclusions therefore do not threaten any comparison this paper draws.
What they establish is that a non-trivial share of both benchmarks is
mislabelled, which is a fact about the benchmarks rather than a
correction to be applied to them.
