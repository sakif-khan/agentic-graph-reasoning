\section{Error Analysis}\label{sec:erroranalysis}

After the label-defect exclusions of \Cref{sec:defects}, AGR's residual
non-hits on the two samples number $262$: $86$ on WebQSP and $176$ on
ComplexWebQuestions. Six of them, one and five, are the surface-form
near-misses of \Cref{sec:protocol}, scoring artefacts rather than
reasoning failures, and were set aside. Every one of the remaining
$256$, $85$ and $171$, was read and labelled by hand against its full
execution trace, so the counts are over a population rather than
estimates from a sample. The reading protocol was fixed before it began:
for each trace, find where the trajectory \emph{first} left the correct
path, and label that point rather than the last visible symptom. Wrong
answers and hedges are kept separate, because a wrong answer is a
reasoning error while a hedge is usually a coverage gap that never
produced a committal claim. A histogram in the supplementary material
gives the census by category. Its largest categories are unsurprising
--- wrong relation choice is the base rate of graph navigation, and
composite claims dominate ComplexWebQuestions, whose template-generated
questions carry conjunctions that WebQSP's search-log questions rarely
do. Questions whose answer is a date, a number, or a superlative fall
into the class \Cref{sec:environment} named as unanswerable here, and on
ComplexWebQuestions the system hedges on them more than three times as
often as it asserts something wrong, $24$ against $7$. Two smaller
categories are reported in full instead, because they generalise past
this architecture.

\subsection{The Echo Attractor}\label{sec:echo}

The most instructive failure mode is one that every grounding check in
this paper passes. The system resolves a real entity, reached through a
real edge, and states a fact that is genuinely true. It is not what was
asked. We name this the \textbf{echo attractor}: a true, well-grounded
entity sitting one hop from the answer in the graph's structure. It
takes two shapes. \emph{Topic confusion} picks a neighbour in a tight
naming cluster, answering that John Glover plays Lionel Luthor when
asked who plays Lex Luthor. \emph{Granularity} picks the right entity
family at the wrong level, answering ``Kingdom of Denmark'' where the
gold answer is ``Denmark'', a genuinely distinct node. A reader may
prefer to call the second a label-granularity problem; we count it
against the system because the two nodes are distinct in the graph and
the question's gold names one of them, and the label-defect pass of
\Cref{sec:defects} is where the other reading was tested.

What makes this a mechanism rather than a residue category is that
nothing went wrong in the parts of the system built to prevent things
going wrong. The right edge exists, is retrieved, and is walked; the
entity returned is present in the environment, and every structural
check certifies it. The failure is entirely in \emph{which} true fact
gets surfaced, which a claim-level check against traversed adjacency
cannot catch by construction, because the claim is true.

\textbf{The consequence for evaluation methodology is larger than the
consequence for this system.} Systems that share a graph and a backbone
converge on the \emph{same} wrong entity for the \emph{same} questions,
and \Cref{sec:defects} measures how often, so any protocol treating
systems as independent observers cannot see it. Consensus filtering,
ensembling, and majority-vote gold repair all take agreement as evidence
of correctness, and here agreement is evidence of a shared bias instead,
so such a protocol will confidently ratify the wrong answer in exactly
the cases it was introduced to catch. The echo attractor accounts for
$13$ of the $256$ failures read. The rate is small and the sample does
not support a precise one: the contribution is the named mechanism and
its methodological consequence, not its frequency at this $n$.

\subsection{Benchmark Label Defects}\label{sec:defects}

The census required a second pass, because a failure against a wrong
gold label is not a failure of the system. A consensus pre-pass flagged
every question where at least three of the five systems agreed on an
answer the benchmark scored wrong, and each was then adjudicated
individually against the graph and the question text. The two counts
must be kept apart. The pre-pass \textbf{flagged} $58$ WebQSP and $47$
ComplexWebQuestions questions; adjudication \textbf{confirmed} a genuine
label defect in $22$ and $19$ of them, which is $5.5\%$ and $4.8\%$ of
each sample, splitting into gold answers that are simply wrong ($12$ and
$17$) and questions whose phrasing admits several defensible answers of
which the benchmark lists one ($10$ and $2$). All $41$ were removed
before the census began, as benchmark artifacts rather than system
failures. The hand-read then found $16$ more that the pre-pass had
missed --- $3$ on WebQSP and $13$ on ComplexWebQuestions --- so
\textbf{$57$ distinct questions} across the two benchmarks turned out to
need correcting. That the census recovered defects a dedicated consensus
pass had missed is itself an argument for reading failures individually
rather than screening them in aggregate.

The gap between flagged and confirmed is not adjudication noise. The
pass flagged $105$ questions and adjudication confirmed $41$, so roughly
three-fifths of what it flagged was several systems converging on the
same wrong entity. The supplementary material reads those $64$ cleared
questions row by row; the triage filed $52$ of the $85$ cleared rows as
the echo mechanism of \Cref{sec:echo} and the rest as relation
confusion, an ignored constraint, or the environment gap. Those rows
also show that the agreeing systems are not independent observers for a
second reason besides the graph: they share one backbone and one
resolver, and the no-retrieval control, which sees no graph at all,
joins the majority of the cleared ComplexWebQuestions rows (shown in the
supplementary material). Convergence there is at least as much a
property of what the backbone believes as of the graph's neighbourhood
structure, and the methodological consequence does not depend on which
cause dominates: had we accepted the pre-pass without per-item
adjudication, we would have ``corrected'' the benchmark toward the
systems' shared bias and reported an inflated accuracy for every system,
including our own.

\textbf{The accuracy tables are not rescored.} \Cref{sec:results}
reports every system exactly as measured over the full samples.
Rescoring against labels we adjudicated ourselves would substitute one
contestable ground truth for another, and the defects cut both ways, so
removing the adjudicated questions moves every system by at most $0.020$
Hits@1 and changes no ranking (recomputed in the supplementary
material). The adjudication was made by one author with the consensus
signal visible, and \Cref{sec:limits-measurement} carries that as the
limitation it is.
