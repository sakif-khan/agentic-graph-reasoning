\section{Aggregate Results and Where the Margin Lives}\label{sec:results}

The same runs are read several times over. This section reports what the
five systems answer correctly and what they spend doing it, then asks
where the margin over the agentic baseline comes from, how the numbers
sit against a published system on the same question sets, how accuracy
varies with the number of hops a question requires, and whether the
entities the systems assert are supported by the graph they navigated.
The aggregate table comes first because the rest of the paper takes it
apart. Where a pass narrows to two systems it says so. Where AGR is not
the best of the five, it says that too.

\subsection{Accuracy}\label{sec:accuracy}

\Cref{tab:main} gives the main matrix. AGR has the highest Hits@1 and F1
in every column. It reaches Hits@1 of $0.755$ on WebQSP against the
agentic baseline's $0.660$, and $0.522$ on ComplexWebQuestions against
$0.435$. The margin over Think-on-Graph is $9.5$ and $8.7$ points. The
per-question F1 gap is wider still, $0.642$ against $0.477$ and $0.469$
against $0.378$, because F1 penalises the extra wrong entities that a
set-intersection hit forgives. All eight paired comparisons against the
four baselines reject at the conventional level. The two against
Think-on-Graph, the only comparisons in which both systems navigate the
same graph with the same model under the same call budget, rest on $76$
questions AGR alone answers against $38$ the other way on WebQSP ($p =
4.8 \times 10^{-4}$), and $86$ against $51$ on ComplexWebQuestions ($p =
3.5 \times 10^{-3}$). That is the aggregate. \Cref{sec:margin} shows
that it is a statement about a budget regime, and \Cref{sec:attribution}
shows which of its components earn their place.

Two features of this table cut against a simple reading. \textbf{Raw
hits mislead on the static graph baseline, and the correction is itself
a finding.} GraphRAG scores $0.340$ on WebQSP, below the no-retrieval
control's $0.453$. That invites the conclusion that the implementation
is broken. Putting the wrong-answer counts beside the hits inverts the
picture. The control asserts on $351$ of $400$ questions and is wrong on
$170$ of them, an \emph{assertion precision} of $51.6\%$, where
assertion precision is the share of questions a system commits on that
it answers correctly. GraphRAG asserts on $177$ and is wrong on $41$, or
$76.8\%$. The mechanism is the prompt. The retrieval baselines are
instructed to answer only from the facts retrieved and to return nothing
otherwise, so they abstain when retrieval misses. The control has no
such constraint and guesses from memory, and on these benchmarks its
guesses are often right. Whether that is pretraining exposure or general
knowledge we did not measure and do not claim. What the control exposes
is that a grounded system can score below an unconstrained one on raw
hits while committing four times fewer falsehoods, and that the raw hit
rate alone would misreport the comparison.

The second is that AGR does not lead on every column. On
ComplexWebQuestions its hedge rate of $23.0\%$ is marginally
\emph{above} Think-on-Graph's $22.5\%$, so it declines to answer
slightly more often there. The accuracy lead is not bought by answering
more freely, and assertion precision on that dataset makes the point
directly. The two systems commit at effectively the same rate, on $308$
and $310$ of $400$ questions. From those near-identical commitments, AGR
is right $209$ times and wrong $99$, against $174$ and $136$ for
Think-on-Graph: $67.9\%$ assertion precision against $56.1\%$. On the
deeper benchmark, the system with an explicit plan, a guided scorer, and
a claim check finds $35$ more answers and asserts $37$ fewer falsehoods
than beam search does, on the same graph, with the same model, from the
same number of commitments.

\subsection{Cost}\label{sec:cost}

AGR reaches those numbers at roughly half the model calls of the agentic
baseline: $6.2$ against $12.8$ on WebQSP and $8.9$ against $18.2$ on
ComplexWebQuestions, ratios of $0.48$ and $0.49$.
\Cref{fig:accuracycost} plots accuracy against cost for all five
systems.

\begin{figure}[!tb]
  \begin{center}
    \input{figures/fig_accuracy_cost}
  \end{center}
  \caption{Accuracy against mean tokens per question, both datasets,
    with cost on a log scale. The single-call baselines cluster at the
    left. The two navigators sit at the right, with AGR above and to
    the right of Think-on-Graph on both panels: it buys its accuracy
    with more tokens per question, not fewer. The halving is in calls,
    which are not plotted.}
  \label{fig:accuracycost}
\end{figure}

The call saving is genuine and the token saving is not. AGR spends
$4{,}511$ mean tokens per question on WebQSP against Think-on-Graph's
$3{,}615$, and $6{,}818$ against $5{,}572$ on ComplexWebQuestions. It is
the \emph{number of round trips} that halves, not the volume of text.
Fewer, larger calls is the accurate description. Which cost binds is a
deployment question. On the token axis of \Cref{fig:accuracycost}
Think-on-Graph is a frontier point rather than a dominated one, since it
buys lower accuracy at a lower token price. On calls it is dominated on
both datasets, and calls are what the shared budget meters. Against
one-shot retrieval, agency costs roughly an order of magnitude more in
tokens. That is the price of the accuracy in \Cref{tab:main}, and it is
stated as a price. Wall-clock time tells the same story in seconds: a
mean of $11.2$ and $17.2$ per question for AGR against $12.9$ and $15.2$
for the baseline, and between $1.0$ and $2.2$ for the three single-call
systems. Read those as magnitudes only. Records replayed entirely from a
response cache are excluded from those means, but between $3.6\%$ and
$15.2\%$ of the two navigators' calls in the remaining records were
still served from the cache, which shortens a cached call to nothing.
The figures are therefore lower bounds and do not rank the two
navigators against each other at the second.

AGR never reaches the shared call cap. Across all $800$ test questions
it hit the $25$-call limit zero times. That is not the same as running
unbounded: its depth cap of \Cref{tab:budget} refuses a deeper expansion
on $25.6\%$ of test questions. The baseline reaches the cap far more
often, and the next subsection shows that this asymmetry, not resolution
quality, is where the aggregate margin lives.

\subsection{Where the Margin Comes From}\label{sec:margin}

The more informative result is \emph{where} the accuracy margin comes
from, and it is not where a reader would assume. Beam search costs width
times depth, so the shared $25$-call cap binds on Think-on-Graph far
more often than on AGR. At width $3$ a full depth costs the baseline up
to thirteen calls (\Cref{sec:baselines}), so the cap admits one full
depth and part of a second, and the reimplementation reached its third
expansion on $6$ of $400$ WebQSP questions and $11$ of $400$ on
ComplexWebQuestions. Its published depth of three is nominal under this
cap. Splitting the questions by whether the cap cut Think-on-Graph off
before it finished separates two things the aggregate confounds: how
good its search is, and whether it can afford to finish that search.
\Cref{tab:togsplit} gives the split, and it reverses the comparison on
one side.

On the questions where the budget lets Think-on-Graph run to completion,
it is \emph{ahead} of AGR: $0.852$ against $0.788$ on WebQSP, and
$0.629$ against $0.607$ on ComplexWebQuestions. The entire aggregate
margin comes from the $29\%$ and $44\%$ of questions on which the cap
truncates it. There its accuracy collapses to $0.197$ and $0.188$, while
AGR still reaches $0.675$ and $0.415$.

The split conditions on the baseline's own outcome, so its two halves
need not be equally hard, and the reading above would be unsafe if the
clipped half were simply the hard half. \Cref{tab:togstrata} therefore
reads the split within hop strata. On WebQSP the clipped half is not
harder for AGR. $81$ of the $117$ clipped questions are single-hop, AGR
scores $0.728$ on them against $0.766$ on the single-hop questions the
baseline finished, and the baseline's fall from $0.846$ to $0.160$
across the same two sets is the cap, not the questions. On
ComplexWebQuestions the halves do differ: AGR scores $0.309$ on the
clipped single-hop questions against $0.609$ on the finished ones, so
there truncation and difficulty are confounded and the affordability
reading is correspondingly weaker. The finished halves are not uniform
either. On ComplexWebQuestions the baseline leads AGR only on single-hop
questions, $0.754$ against $0.609$, and trails on two-hop, $0.561$
against $0.610$. That is the planner asymmetry of \Cref{sec:planner}
seen from the other side.

The correct reading of this is affordability, not resolution quality.
Exhaustive beam search finds answers well when it is allowed to finish.
Guided search finds them almost as well and finishes within a budget the
beam search cannot. On a fixed spend the second is the better
architecture. The first would win if spend were unconstrained. That is a
claim about operating regimes rather than about reasoning power, and it
is narrower than the aggregate table alone would suggest. The cap of
$25$ calls is itself a choice, and a larger one would move the split.
The clip rates are reported so a reader can see how far, and a sweep of
the cap is the first experiment \Cref{sec:outlook} names.

\textbf{One asymmetry bounds how far that reading extends}, and it is
the candidate-width difference named in \Cref{sec:baselines}. Both
navigators call the same tools, but they truncate what those tools
return at different widths. Think-on-Graph keeps the first $40$
relations per entity and the first $20$ neighbours per relation, the
pruning widths of the reimplemented algorithm, against AGR's $300$ and
$200$. The relation list is returned sorted by \emph{descending} fanout,
so a binding cut discards the low-fanout tail. That tail is where the
specific relation answering a specific question tends to sit, since a
high-fanout relation is rarely the edge that resolves a particular
entity. Over the committed tool logs the $40$-relation cut binds on
$31.6\%$ of the $1{,}651$ entities Think-on-Graph expanded and the
$20$-neighbour cut on $32.8\%$ of its $7{,}992$ neighbour calls, against
$1$ of AGR's $3{,}097$ expanded entities and $3.3\%$ of its neighbour
calls.

The affordability conclusion survives this, because a thinner candidate
pool cannot explain why a system runs \emph{out of calls}. What it
bounds is the other half of the split. The $0.852$ and $0.629$ the
baseline reaches on the questions it finishes were measured while it
searched a narrower pool than AGR. They are therefore a \textbf{lower
bound} on what an equal-width reimplementation would reach, not an
estimate of it. And since the baseline is already ahead of AGR on those
questions, equalising the widths would be expected to widen that lead
rather than close it. Beam width and depth follow the original paper.
The candidate widths beneath them do not, and equalising them is the
first change we would make in a follow-up. That re-run would not vary
one thing, though. Wider candidate sets make each pruning call dearer,
so equal widths would also raise the baseline's clip rate. That moves
the boundary between the two halves of \Cref{tab:togsplit} at the same
time as it changes what the search resolves within each. The two effects
have to be read apart, which means reporting that split again rather
than an aggregate in which they cancel or compound invisibly.

The breadth of AGR's answers is checked rather than assumed, because a
metric that scores a hit on any match rewards naming more entities. On
the WebQSP questions it answers, AGR names $3.37$ entities against
Think-on-Graph's $2.56$, and its entity precision over those same
answered questions is $0.760$ against $0.703$. On ComplexWebQuestions
the figures are $1.54$ against $1.40$ entities and $0.629$ against
$0.520$ precision. If AGR bought breadth without accuracy, precision
would fall as the entity count rose. It does not fall.

\subsection{Positioning Against Published RoG Results}\label{sec:rog}

Two facts in this paper invite a comparison that the controlled protocol
above cannot make. The environment of \Cref{sec:environment} is built
from the per-question subgraphs that the RoG distribution publishes, and
\Cref{sec:rel-agentic} surveys RoG~\cite{luo2024reasoning} as the
closest published system in the path-retrieval family. A reader who
notices both will ask how the two systems compare. The aggregate answer
does not favour this work, and \Cref{tab:rog} gives it before the
qualifications rather than after them.

\textbf{RoG leads on all four figures, and the margin is not sampling
noise.} AGR's $95\%$ intervals on Hits@1 are $[71.3, 79.7]$ on WebQSP
and $[47.5, 57.0]$ on ComplexWebQuestions. RoG's $85.7$ and $62.6$ fall
outside both. Four differences bound what the table establishes. The
first is decisive and the other three are ordinary. \textbf{RoG is
trained on these benchmarks; AGR is trained on nothing.} RoG's backbone
is instruction-tuned on the training splits of both benchmarks, which
hold $2{,}826$ and $27{,}639$ questions in the distribution used here.
AGR's backbone is frozen and prompted, and it has seen no question from
either benchmark before evaluation. The comparison is between a
supervised system and a zero-shot one, which is a difference in kind
rather than in degree. \textbf{The backbones differ.} RoG fine-tunes
LLaMA2-Chat-7B. \Cref{sec:baselines} holds the backbone constant
precisely because it accounts for much of the spread in cross-paper
tables, and it cannot be held constant against a system whose
contribution \emph{is} its fine-tuned weights. \textbf{The populations
differ.} RoG reports over the full test splits. Our figures are over
stratified $400$-question samples of the same splits, which estimate the
same quantity, with the intervals above as their uncertainty. RoG
publishes no interval, so the gap can be bounded from one side only.
\textbf{The substrate differs in one respect.} RoG retrieves within each
question's own published subgraph, while AGR navigates the union of all
of them, so the two systems see the same triples but AGR sees every
other question's triples as well.

What the comparison settles is AGR's place in the published landscape: a
training-free system reaching $75.5$ and $52.2$ against a supervised
system's $85.7$ and $62.6$ on the same question sets, with no in-domain
examples. What it does not settle is anything this paper measures.
Neither the component-level ablation of \Cref{sec:attribution} nor the
failure census of \Cref{sec:erroranalysis} has a counterpart in RoG's
evaluation, and closing the gap in \Cref{tab:rog} would inform neither.

\subsection{Accuracy by Hop Depth}\label{sec:hops}

\Cref{fig:hopstrata} stratifies by the number of hops separating a topic
entity from the gold answer.

\begin{figure}[!tb]
  \begin{center}
    \input{figures/fig_hop_strata}
  \end{center}
  \caption{Hits@1 by hop stratum. On ComplexWebQuestions (right) AGR is
    the only system whose accuracy rises monotonically with hop count.
    The WebQSP three-hop stratum holds four questions and no weight
    should be placed on it.}
  \label{fig:hopstrata}
\end{figure}

On ComplexWebQuestions, AGR is the only system whose accuracy
\emph{rises} with hop count: $0.46$ at one hop, $0.55$ at two, $0.57$ at
three or more, over strata of $137$, $211$, and $49$ questions. The last
step sits well inside the interval of a $49$-question stratum, so the
claim is the direction across the three strata, not the size of any one
step. Every other system ends below where it started. That phrasing is
deliberate, and the weaker alternative is the true one. Think-on-Graph
does not decay monotonically. It falls from $0.53$ to $0.37$ and then
partly recovers to $0.45$, which is a different shape from a steady
decline, and we report it as such. Think-on-Graph also beats AGR on that
dataset's one-hop stratum, $0.53$ against $0.46$. \Cref{sec:planner}
identifies the mechanism: decomposition costs accuracy on questions that
do not need decomposing, and that stratum is full of them.

The static graph baseline's decay across these strata is deliberately
\emph{not} offered as evidence for the paradigm, because its retrieval
radius confounds it. A one-hop retriever cannot contain a two-hop chain,
so part of that decline measures the radius rather than static retrieval
as such. The evidence that it is a radius effect is in the strata
themselves. GraphRAG scores $0.44$ on WebQSP's two-hop stratum, where
those questions are largely mediator paths a one-hop expansion does
reach, against $0.16$ on the ComplexWebQuestions two-hop stratum, where
the chains are genuine compositions it cannot reach at all. A retriever
failing uniformly on depth would not show that gap.

A rising curve is what decomposition would predict: it converts one hard
question into several easy ones, so the questions that punish
single-shot retrieval hardest are the ones an agent that re-plans
between hops should handle best. The curve is consistent with that
mechanism rather than evidence for it. \Cref{sec:planner} finds that
removing the planner costs accuracy on this dataset in the direction the
mechanism predicts, and that the loss does not reach significance at the
sample available. The WebQSP panel carries no comparable evidence. Its
three-hop stratum contains four questions, which is why the claim is
scoped to one dataset.

\subsection{Groundedness}\label{sec:groundedness}

Across both datasets AGR asserted $1{,}709$ entities, and \textbf{zero
of them were ungrounded}. Every entity it named exists in the
environment and is reachable from a topic entity within the hop cap. The
parametric control asserted $1{,}001$ entities, of which $22.1\%$ failed
that test. Two qualifications come before any reading of that contrast.
The property is close to guaranteed by construction for any system that
copies its entities out of tool results, because every node a tool
returns is in the environment and was reached from a topic entity. And
\textbf{it belongs to graph navigation, not to the verification layer.}
Think-on-Graph also reaches exactly zero ungrounded assertions, on
$1{,}265$ entities, without any claim-checking component whatsoever.
Whatever the verification layer contributes, it is not this number. What
the comparison establishes is that an agent which can only name entities
it retrieved cannot fabricate one, and that this property follows from
the retrieval discipline rather than from any check applied afterwards.

The semantic tier asks the harder question of whether an asserted entity
is supported \emph{as the answer}, and it separates the systems far
less. \Cref{tab:tier2} gives every cell rather than a selection, because
the ordering differs by dataset and any subset of it misleads.

rather than quote the two comparators it happens to beat.

Neither gap survives the sample. At $n = 60$ per cell a $5$-point
difference sits well inside the interval, as does Vector-RAG's $1.7$,
and no conclusion in this paper rests on either. What the tier
establishes is the level rather than the ranking, and the level is the
more useful finding: \textbf{zero structural hallucination coexists with
between a third and nearly two-thirds of assertions failing a strict
semantic-support test}, for every system, including the two that are
structurally perfect. Semantic support is hard for the paradigm, not for
one system within it.
