\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Large language models answer factual questions fluently, and often
wrongly. The errors are systematic rather than random, and a fabricated
answer reads in the same register as a correct one, so they are hard to
catch from the output alone~\cite{huang2025survey}. The problem sharpens
once an answer requires composing several facts. A wrong resolution at
the first hop rarely announces itself downstream, and a correctness
check on the final answer never sees the intermediate steps.

Retrieval-augmented generation~\cite{lewis2020retrieval} grounds the
model in retrieved text, but it retrieves once, before reasoning begins,
and the passage holding an intermediate entity need not resemble the
question at all. A knowledge graph~\cite{bollacker2008freebase} turns
composition into traversal. Static graph retrieval~\cite{edge2024local}
still fetches a neighbourhood in one shot and cannot know what the
second hop will need. Agentic systems close that gap by interleaving
retrieval with reasoning: the model takes one step, observes what it
found, and decides where to look
next~\cite{sun2024think,chen2024plan,luo2024reasoning,luo2025kbqao1}.
\Cref{sec:related} traces that line of work.

These systems are assemblies. A typical one decomposes the question into
sub-goals, scores candidate edges with the model, backtracks from dead
ends, and checks or rewrites its own output. The literature reports the
assembly as a whole. When it outperforms a static baseline, three things
could have produced the gain, and the report rarely separates them: a
component, the budget regime the comparison ran under, or the labels of
the benchmark itself. Later work then inherits the entire assembly on
faith, along with its cost. The exception shows why the instrument
matters. KBQA-o1's own ablation removes its tree search and GrailQA F1
falls by thirty points~\cite{luo2025kbqao1}. That attribution exists
because the authors took the system apart. This paper applies the same
instrument to an agentic knowledge-graph question-answering system built
so that each of its components can be removed cleanly, and reads all
three sources of the gain.

The instrument is \textbf{Agentic Graph Reasoning} (AGR), an explicit
state machine over a constrained, deterministic graph tool API. A
program orchestrates the loop, and the language model is consulted only
at bounded decision points. Its four removable components are a
\emph{planner} that decomposes the question into an ordered chain of
sub-objectives, a hybrid \emph{edge scorer} that blends embedding
similarity with a batched model judgement, a \emph{backtracker} that
restores an earlier frontier and bans the edges that led away from it,
and a \emph{structural verification layer} that decomposes each draft
answer into atomic claims and checks them against the traversed triples
before the answer is emitted. The last is the component the system was
designed around, and we state its reach at the width the measurements
support. Its two graph routes ask whether an edge joins a claim's
endpoints, not whether it is the edge the claim asserts, so a claim that
X is Y's mother is certified by an edge recording that X is Y's child.
Only the traversed-adjacency route attaches evidence.
\Cref{sec:verification,sec:limits-contribution} bound it further.

The benchmarks are WebQSP~\cite{yih2016value} and
ComplexWebQuestions~\cite{talmor2018web}, $400$ questions from each,
over a Freebase-derived environment of $2.59$ million entities and
$8.31$ million triples. On them AGR reaches Hits@1 of $0.755$ and
$0.522$ and leads four baselines that share its backbone and its call
budget. That number is where the analysis starts rather than where it
ends. Against the agentic baseline, a reimplementation of
Think-on-Graph~\cite{sun2024think} on the same tools, the entire margin
comes from the $29\%$ and $44\%$ of questions on which the shared
$25$-call cap truncates the baseline, and, read a second way, from the
questions on which the reimplementation's own candidate cut discarded a
relation on the path to the answer. Where neither binds, the baseline is
ahead on both datasets (\Cref{sec:margin}). The ablation finds one
component with a detectable effect, and the effect runs the wrong way.
Removing the planner improves WebQSP F1 by $0.083$ while cutting tokens
by $31\%$, because decomposing a question with no compositional
structure discards the context that would have answered it.
Backtracking, model scoring, and claim verification show no effect at
the power available, and \Cref{sec:power} states the smallest effect
each test could have caught. The failure census finds label defects on
$5.5\%$ and $4.8\%$ of the sampled questions, and names a failure that
every grounding check passes and that consensus-based label repair
cannot see.

\paragraph{Contributions} This paper contributes:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item A \textbf{controlled attribution protocol} for agentic KGQA: one
    environment, one frozen backbone, one budget meter, metrics and
    ablation conditions fixed before any test question ran, paired
    significance tests, and a minimum detectable effect reported beside
    every null (\Cref{sec:setup,sec:power}).
  \item \textbf{Component attribution} for a four-component agentic
    system. Removing the planner \emph{improves} accuracy on the
    shallower benchmark while reducing token use, and the per-stratum
    breakdown gives the mechanism. The other three components, the
    claim-verification layer among them, show no detectable effect, and
    the nulls are reported at the same length as the effect
    (\Cref{sec:attribution}).
    \item The \textbf{location of the aggregate margin} over the agentic
    baseline. It lies entirely on the questions where the shared call
    cap truncated the baseline or the baseline's candidate cut discarded
    a gold-path relation, both read from the logs, and the split is
    read within hop strata. Where neither binds, the baseline is ahead.
    The margin is a fact about the comparison's regime, not about the
    architecture (\Cref{sec:margin}).
  \item A \textbf{hand-read failure census} that names the \emph{echo
    attractor}, reports which systems converge on it, and quantifies
    label defects in both benchmarks together with their effect on the
    accuracy tables (\Cref{sec:erroranalysis}).
  \item \textbf{AGR} itself, released with its prompts, per-question
    records, and analysis scripts, and its verification layer reported
    as it behaves: how often it fires, how far the record can attribute
    an acceptance to a route, and what the output contract does and does
    not guarantee (\Cref{sec:framework,sec:limits-contribution}).
\end{enumerate}

\Cref{sec:related} positions the study against prior work.
\Cref{sec:framework} specifies the instrument. \Cref{sec:setup}
describes the environment and the protocol. \Cref{sec:results} reports
the aggregate measurements and locates the margin.
\Cref{sec:attribution} attributes the measurements to components,
\Cref{sec:erroranalysis} reads what remains, and \Cref{sec:discussion}
states the limits.
