\section{Error Analysis}\label{sec:erroranalysis}

After the label-defect exclusions of \Cref{sec:defects}, AGR's residual
failures on the two samples number $262$: $86$ on WebQSP and $176$ on
ComplexWebQuestions. Each was to be read and labelled by hand against
its full execution trace, and $256$ of them were, $85$ and $171$. The
six the census did not reach, one WebQSP and five ComplexWebQuestions
wrong answers, were missed by both labelling passes and are listed in
the repository. The counts below are therefore counts over the $256$
read rather than estimates from a sample, and no category is
extrapolated. The reading protocol was fixed before it began. For each
trace, find where the trajectory \emph{first} left the correct path, and
label that point rather than the last visible symptom. Wrong answers and
hedges are kept separate throughout, because the two are not the same
kind of failure. A wrong answer is a reasoning error. A hedge is usually
a coverage gap that never produced a committal claim.
\Cref{fig:failures} gives the census.

The bulk of the distribution is unsurprising. Choosing the wrong
relation to follow is the base rate of graph navigation, and it is the
largest category overall. Composite claims, in which an unverifiable
component drags down a verifiable one, are nearly absent on WebQSP and
the largest category on ComplexWebQuestions, because that benchmark's
template-generated questions carry conjunctions and constraints that
WebQSP's search-log questions rarely do. The same construction explains
the third-largest category. Questions whose answer is a date, a number,
or a superlative fall into the class \Cref{sec:environment} named as
unanswerable in this environment, and on ComplexWebQuestions the system
hedges on them more than three times as often as it asserts something
wrong, $24$ against $7$. That is the behaviour the drafting prompt's
grounding provisions were written to produce, visible in the one place
where the environment guarantees the system cannot succeed. Two smaller
categories are reported in full instead, because they generalise past
this architecture.

\subsection{The Echo Attractor}\label{sec:echo}

The most instructive failure mode is one that every grounding check in
this paper passes. The system resolves a real entity, reached through a
real edge, and states a fact that is genuinely true. It is not what was
asked. We name this the \textbf{echo attractor}: a true, well-grounded
entity sitting one hop from the answer in the graph's structure.

It takes two shapes. \emph{Topic confusion} picks a neighbour in a tight
naming cluster. Asked who plays Lex Luthor on \emph{Smallville}, the
system answers that John Glover plays Lionel Luthor, a different
character in the same fictional family, with three further Luthor
variants surfacing in the trace before the run gives up.
\emph{Granularity} picks the right entity family at the wrong level.
Asked for a country, the system answers ``Kingdom of Denmark'' where the
gold answer is ``Denmark'', a genuinely distinct node, the formal
sovereign-state entity rather than the common-name one. A reader may
prefer to call the second shape a label-granularity problem rather than
a system error. We count it against the system because the two nodes are
distinct in the graph and the question's gold names one of them, and the
label-defect pass of \Cref{sec:defects} is where the other reading was
tested, question by question.

What makes this a mechanism rather than a residue category is that
nothing went wrong in the parts of the system built to prevent things
going wrong. The right edge exists, is retrieved, and is walked. The
entity returned is verifiably present in the environment. Every
structural check certifies it. The failure is entirely in \emph{which}
true fact gets surfaced, which is architecturally distinct both from
following a wrong edge and from the fact being absent. A claim-level
check against traversed adjacency cannot catch it by construction. The
claim is true.

\textbf{The consequence for evaluation methodology is larger than the
consequence for this system.} Systems that share a graph and a backbone
converge on the \emph{same} wrong entity for the \emph{same} questions,
and \Cref{sec:defects} measures how often. Any protocol that treats
systems as independent observers therefore cannot see it. Consensus
filtering, ensembling, and majority-vote gold repair all take agreement
as evidence of correctness, and here agreement is evidence of a shared
bias instead. Such a protocol will confidently ratify the wrong answer
in exactly the cases it was introduced to catch.

The echo attractor accounts for $13$ of the $256$ failures read. The
rate is small and the sample does not support a precise one. The
contribution is the named mechanism and its methodological consequence,
not its frequency at this $n$.

\subsection{Benchmark Label Defects}\label{sec:defects}

The census required a second pass, because a failure against a wrong
gold label is not a failure of the system. A consensus pre-pass flagged
every question where at least three of the five systems agreed on an
answer the benchmark scored wrong. Each flagged question was then
adjudicated individually against the graph and the question text.

The two counts must be kept apart. The pre-pass \textbf{flagged} $58$
WebQSP and $47$ ComplexWebQuestions questions. Adjudication
\textbf{confirmed} a genuine label defect in $22$ and $19$ of them,
which is $5.5\%$ and $4.8\%$ of each sample. Confirmed defects split
into two kinds: gold answers that are simply wrong ($12$ and $17$), and
questions whose phrasing admits several defensible answers of which the
benchmark lists one ($10$ and $2$). All $41$ were removed before the
census began, as benchmark artifacts rather than system failures.

The hand-read then found $16$ more that the pre-pass had missed --- $3$
on WebQSP and $13$ on ComplexWebQuestions --- and filed them under its
own gold-noise and ambiguous-question labels. So \textbf{$57$ distinct
questions} across the two benchmarks turned out to need correcting. That
the census recovered defects a dedicated consensus pass had missed is
itself an argument for reading failures individually rather than
screening them in aggregate.

The gap between flagged and confirmed is not adjudication noise. The
pass flagged $105$ questions and adjudication confirmed $41$. So roughly
three-fifths of what it flagged was not a bad label at all but several
systems converging on the same wrong entity. \Cref{tab:consensus} reads
those $64$ cleared questions row by row, where a row is one consensus
answer and a question can carry more than one. The triage filed $52$ of
the $85$ cleared rows as the echo mechanism of \Cref{sec:echo} and the
rest as relation confusion, an ignored constraint, or the environment
gap, so the gap between flagged and confirmed is mostly, not entirely,
the echo attractor. The table also shows that the agreeing systems are
not independent observers for a second reason besides the graph: they
share one backbone and one resolver. On ComplexWebQuestions the
no-retrieval control, which sees no graph at all, is among the agreeing
systems on $25$ of the $31$ cleared rows and on $11$ of the $16$ echo
rows, so there the convergence is at least as much a property of what
the backbone believes as of the graph's neighbourhood structure. On
WebQSP the control joins $15$ of $54$ rows and $13$ of $36$ echo rows,
and the two navigators agree with each other on $20$ of the $36$. Of the
$13$ rows on which all five systems agreed, $9$ were cleared as not a
defect. The methodological consequence does not depend on which cause
dominates. A protocol that takes agreement as evidence of correctness
ratifies the shared answer either way, and had we accepted the pre-pass
without per-item adjudication, we would have ``corrected'' the benchmark
toward the systems' shared bias and reported an inflated accuracy for
every system, including our own.

\textbf{The accuracy tables are not rescored.} \Cref{sec:results}
reports every system exactly as measured over the full samples, for two
reasons. Rescoring against labels we adjudicated ourselves would
substitute one contestable ground truth for another. And the defects cut
both ways. A broken label can just as easily hand a system a hit as deny
it one, and $10$ of the $22$ WebQSP exclusions and $4$ of the $19$ on
ComplexWebQuestions are questions AGR scored as hits against a label now
judged defective.

That symmetry also bounds how much the exclusions could matter, which is
worth stating because the defect rate on its own invites a much larger
inference. Recomputing every cell of \Cref{tab:main} with the
adjudicated questions removed moves each system by between $+0.001$ and
$+0.020$ Hits@1 and leaves the ranking on both datasets unchanged. That
shift is an order of magnitude below the $5.5\%$ and $4.8\%$ defect
rates, because removing a question the system got right subtracts from
the numerator as well as the denominator. The exclusions therefore do
not threaten any comparison this paper draws. What they do establish is
that a non-trivial share of both benchmarks is mislabelled, which is a
fact about the benchmarks rather than a correction to be applied to
them. The adjudication was made by one author with the consensus signal
visible, and \Cref{sec:limits-measurement} carries that as the
limitation it is.
