% ===================== 2 =====================
\begin{frame}{The problem}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.54\textwidth}
      Language models answer factual questions \alert{fluently}.

      \vspace{4mm}
      They answer just as fluently when they \alert{do not hold the fact}.

      \vspace{4mm}
      Nothing in the wording separates the two.
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.44\textwidth}
      \begin{block}{The gap}
        Fluency and factuality are produced by the same mechanism, so the
        output gives the reader no signal about which one they are getting.
      \end{block}
      \vspace{3mm}
      A system that is confidently wrong is worse than one that says it
      does not know.
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \takeaway{The failure is not visible in the answer.}
\end{frame}


% ===================== 3 =====================
\begin{frame}{What a hallucination is}
  Output that is fluent, confident, and backed by \alert{no source the
  model can point to}.
  % The two blocks sat 34pt below the lead-in and 9pt above the line under
  % them, because a columns environment and a block each contribute their
  % own skip above and neither contributes one below. Measured off the
  % page and split evenly: 12.4pt comes off the top and goes back on at
  % the bottom, so the pair is centred in its band and the frame's total
  % height is unchanged. Negative here removes part of that inherited
  % skip; it leaves 21.7pt of clear space, and the overprint rule in
  % check_slides.py is what stands behind that claim.
  \vspace{-3.9pt}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.47\textwidth}
      \begin{block}{Factually wrong}
        \agrraggedright
        It contradicts the world: \emph{``Ada Lovelace was born in
        Paris.''} To catch it you need the true answer already --- the
        thing we are trying to produce.
      \end{block}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.47\textwidth}
      \begin{block}{Unsupported}
        \agrraggedright
        It may even be true, but nothing the system retrieved says so. To
        catch it you need \alert{only what was retrieved} --- and we have
        that.
      \end{block}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \vspace{18.1pt}
  Identical from the outside. What differs is what it takes to
  \emph{catch} them.
  \takeaway{A retrieval system can only police the second --- so that is the
    one this thesis attacks, and the only one it claims.}
\end{frame}

% ===================== 4 =====================
\begin{frame}{Where it comes from}
  Not a bug that escaped testing --- a consequence of how these models are
  built, which is why the response has to be architectural.
  \vspace{4mm}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.31\textwidth}
      \begin{block}{The training data}
        Errors and thin coverage, reproduced faithfully.
      \end{block}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.33\textwidth}
      \begin{block}{The model}
        Knowledge is spread across the weights with \alert{no index}, so
        nothing separates a memorised fact from a plausible continuation.
      \end{block}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.31\textwidth}
      \begin{block}{The prompt}
        What we put in the context window --- and what we leave out of it.
      \end{block}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  % "Generating text and recalling a fact are the same operation" claimed
  % more than Sec 1.1.1 does. The book says the model holds no reliable
  % internal *signal* separating the two; interpretability work on
  % fact-recall circuits can be read against the stronger form, and this
  % slide does not need it. Same machinery, identical output -- which is
  % the part the thesis actually relies on, since the output is all a
  % downstream system sees.
  \takeaway{Recalling a fact and inventing one run through the same
    machinery --- and the output looks identical either way.}
\end{frame}

% ===================== 5 =====================
\begin{frame}{Which of the three can we act on?}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.52\textwidth}
      \begin{block}{Only the third}
        The other two need a curated corpus or a retrained model, and
        neither is available to most people deploying one of these systems.
      \end{block}
      \vspace{3mm}
      Controlling what enters the context window --- and checking what
      comes back out against it --- is a \alert{systems} problem.
      That is this thesis.
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.44\textwidth}
      \begin{block}{And multi-hop compounds it}
        When an answer needs two facts chained, a wrong first step is
        never revisited: the rest of the chain proceeds from a false
        premise, just as fluently.
      \end{block}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \takeaway{Fix what goes in, then check what comes out against it --- before
    anything is emitted.}
\end{frame}

% ===================== 6 =====================
\begin{frame}{What a knowledge graph is}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.52\textwidth}
      Facts are stored as \alert{triples} --- head, relation, tail:
      \vspace{3mm}

      \hspace{3mm}\textsf{(Ada Lovelace, place\_of\_birth, London)}
      \vspace{3mm}

      A question needing two of them \emph{chained} is \alert{multi-hop}.
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.44\textwidth}
      \begin{block}{Why a graph at all}
        Every fact has an address. A claim can then be checked by looking
        for an edge --- rather than by asking the model whether it believes
        itself.
      \end{block}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \takeaway{Each step depends on the last, so a wrong first hop is never
    seen again.}
\end{frame}

% ===================== 7 =====================
\begin{frame}{The complication: mediator nodes}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.52\textwidth}
      Freebase stores any fact with more than two participants as a
      \alert{node}, not an edge.
      \vspace{3mm}

      ``Rainn Wilson played Dwight in \textsf{The Office}'' is \alert{one
      node} joining three things --- series, character, actor.
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.44\textwidth}
      \begin{block}{What that costs}
        $63.7\%$ of this graph's nodes are mediators, and they carry
        \alert{no name}.
        \smallskip

        A path that reads as two hops is often three.
      \end{block}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \takeaway{Nothing in the graph is called \textsf{plays}: the relation a
    question names is rarely the relation the graph stores.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Research questions}
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item[\textbf{RQ1}] Does agentic navigation improve multi-hop factual
      accuracy --- and does the advantage \alert{grow with hop count}?
      \vspace{2mm}
    \item[\textbf{RQ2}] Given a system that already navigates the graph, what
      does a claim-level check against traversed triples \alert{contribute}?
      \vspace{2mm}
    \item[\textbf{RQ3}] Which components earn their cost, in accuracy and in
      \alert{tokens}?
  \end{enumerate}
  \vspace{3mm}\sinkfoot
  {\small RQ2 is posed as \emph{what does it contribute}, not \emph{does it
    reduce hallucination}. The answer has several parts, and a yes/no framing
    would hide them.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Structural Verification Layer}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.55\textwidth}
      Before anything is emitted:
      \begin{enumerate}
        \item Draft answer is split into \alert{atomic claims}
        \item Each claim is checked against the \alert{triples actually
          traversed}
        \item Claims grounded by the traversal keep those triples as
          \alert{evidence}
        \item Unsupported claims trigger \alert{targeted re-exploration}, or
          are \alert{dropped}
      \end{enumerate}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.42\textwidth}
      \begin{block}{The behaviour this buys}
        The system \textbf{hedges rather than asserts}, and carries its
        evidence with the answer.
      \end{block}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \takeaway{This is the contribution. The next slide is what it does
    \emph{not} do.}
\end{frame}

% ===================== 14 =====================
% The bound used to sit in a block on the same slide as the four steps,
% under a heading that read as a footnote to them. It is not a footnote:
% it is the property the supervisor's first issue is about, and it is now
% the whole of a slide with the contribution's name in its title.
\begin{frame}{What \emph{structural} means --- and what it does not}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.5\textwidth}
      \begin{block}{What it means}
        The check is against the graph the agent \alert{walked}, not the
        model's judgement of its own output.
      \end{block}
      \vspace{2mm}
      \begin{block}{What it does not mean}
        \small
        It tests \alert{adjacency}: whether an edge joins the claim's
        endpoints, not whether it is the edge the claim \emph{names}, and
        not its direction. A \emph{mother} claim passes on a \emph{child}
        edge --- only route three reads the relation.
      \end{block}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.46\textwidth}
      \begin{block}{The other honest limit}
        \small
        A claim can be \emph{true} and still be the wrong answer.
        Structural grounding cannot catch that --- see the echo attractor
        later.
      \end{block}
      \vspace{2mm}
      {\small ``Its evidence'' is narrower than it sounds, too:
      \alert{one route of three} records any, and only inside the run.\par}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

        ToG prunes to $40$/$20$ candidates per step, AGR to $300$/$200$.

      \end{block}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.44\textwidth}
      \begin{block}{Why a parametric control}
        These benchmarks predate current models and may be partly
        memorised. Without it we could not separate retrieval from recall.
      \end{block}
