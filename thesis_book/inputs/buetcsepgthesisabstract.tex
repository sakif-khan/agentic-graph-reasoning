Large language models answer factual questions with real confidence, and they
tend to sound just as confident when the fact is not actually there.
Retrieval-augmented generation helps by grounding the model in retrieved text,
but the retrieval happens once and up front, before any reasoning starts, so a
question that spans several facts has to be answered from whatever that first
query brought back. Systems that navigate a knowledge graph do better by
interleaving retrieval with reasoning. Even they, however, send out whatever
their final generation call produces, and nothing checks the answer's claims
before it reaches the user.

To close that gap we propose Agentic Graph Reasoning (AGR), a knowledge-graph
question-answering framework in which an agent plans sub-objectives and walks
the graph through a constrained, deterministic tool API. Its distinguishing
component is a Structural Verification Layer: before the answer is emitted, it
splits the draft into atomic claims and checks each one against the triples the
agent actually traversed. Claims it cannot ground are re-explored or withdrawn,
so AGR is more inclined to hedge than to assert, and it returns every answer
paired with the triples that support it.

We evaluate AGR on a Freebase-derived environment of $2{,}592{,}892$ entities and
$8{,}309{,}194$ triples, over $400$ certified questions from each of WebQSP and
ComplexWebQuestions, against four baselines: parametric answering, vector
retrieval, GraphRAG, and an agentic navigator. All five systems share one frozen
backbone and the same call budget, so the differences between them come down to
architecture rather than model capacity.

AGR reaches Hits@1 of $0.755$ and F1 of $0.642$ on WebQSP, and $0.522$ and $0.469$
on ComplexWebQuestions, ahead of every baseline. That includes the agentic
navigator, which scores $0.660$ and $0.435$ Hits@1 and actually leads on the
questions where its call budget lets it finish, but which gets clipped on $29\%$
and $44\%$ of all questions. AGR does all of this at half the language-model calls.
Across both datasets it asserts no ungrounded entity in $1{,}709$ assertions,
against $22.1\%$ of the $1{,}001$ made by the parametric control, a property we
attribute to graph navigation itself rather than to the verification layer. On
ComplexWebQuestions it is the only system whose accuracy rises with hop count;
every other system ends below where it started.

A four-condition ablation with paired significance testing finds an accuracy
effect in only one component, and the sign is the opposite of what we expected:
removing the planner \emph{improves} WebQSP by $0.083$ F1 at $p = 0.006$ and cuts
token use by $31\%$, while trending the other way on ComplexWebQuestions.
Decomposition, in other words, should be gated on question structure rather than
applied unconditionally. We read all $259$ remaining failures by hand, and the
characteristic error of a graph navigator turns out not to be invention but the
\emph{echo attractor}: a real, grounded entity one hop from the answer, which
any grounding check will pass because it is genuinely true, and which different
systems fall into together. The same pass confirms $57$ questions where the
benchmark, not the system, was wrong.
