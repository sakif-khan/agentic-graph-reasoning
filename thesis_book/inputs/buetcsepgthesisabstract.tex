Large language models answer factual questions fluently. When they do not hold
the fact, they get it wrong just as fluently. Retrieval-augmented
generation grounds them in retrieved text, but it retrieves once, before
reasoning begins. A multi-hop question must then be answered from whatever that
single query returned.

This thesis presents Agentic Graph Reasoning (AGR), which interleaves retrieval
with reasoning instead. An agent navigates a knowledge graph through a
constrained, deterministic tool API. It breaks the question into sub-objectives,
expands a scored frontier, and backtracks when a branch stops paying. Its
distinguishing component is a Structural Verification Layer. The layer splits the
draft answer into atomic claims and checks each one against the triples the agent
actually traversed, before the answer is emitted. Claims that cannot be grounded
are re-explored or withdrawn. The system therefore hedges rather than asserts,
and every answer arrives with the triples that support it.

AGR is evaluated on a Freebase-derived environment of $2{,}592{,}892$ entities
and $8{,}309{,}194$ triples, over $400$ certified questions from each of WebQSP
and ComplexWebQuestions. Five systems are compared: parametric answering, vector
retrieval, GraphRAG, an agentic-navigation baseline, and AGR. All five run on one
frozen backbone under the same budget, so that architecture rather than model
capacity accounts for the differences.

AGR reaches Hits@1 of $0.755$ and F1 of $0.642$ on WebQSP, and $0.522$ and
$0.469$ on ComplexWebQuestions. That is ahead of every baseline, including the
agentic one at $0.660$ and $0.435$, at half the language-model calls. AGR
asserts no ungrounded entity across $1{,}709$ assertions, against $27.1\%$ for
parametric answering. The thesis attributes
that property to graph navigation itself rather than to the verification layer.
On ComplexWebQuestions, AGR's accuracy rises with hop count while every other
system decays.

A four-condition ablation with paired significance testing finds one component
with a detectable accuracy effect, and its sign is not the expected one. Removing
the planner \emph{improves} WebQSP by $0.083$ F1 at $p = 0.006$ and cuts tokens
by $30\%$. On ComplexWebQuestions it trends the other way. Decomposition should
therefore be gated on question structure rather than applied unconditionally. A
census of all $259$ remaining failures names the characteristic error of a
graph-navigating system. It is not invention but the \emph{echo attractor}: a
real, grounded entity one hop from the answer, which any grounding check passes
because it is true. The census also finds $59$ questions where the
benchmark was wrong rather than the system. Consensus against gold is dominated by
that same attractor rather than by annotation error. Rescoring on majority
agreement would hide this failure rather than correct it.
