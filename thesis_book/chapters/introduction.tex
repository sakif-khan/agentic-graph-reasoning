\chapter{Introduction}\label{ch:intro}

Large language models answer factual questions fluently. Often enough, they
answer them incorrectly. The failure is not random noise. It is systematic and
confident, and it is hard to detect from the output alone, because a fabricated
answer is written in the same register as a correct one. The rate rises sharply
when the answer requires composing several facts rather than recalling one. This
thesis closes that gap in two steps. It gives the model a symbolic knowledge
graph to walk through. It then checks what the model is about to assert, before
it asserts it.

This chapter motivates the problem and surveys why the obvious remedies are
incomplete. It then states the problem and the research questions, summarises the
contributions, and sets out how the rest of the document is organised.

\section{Hallucination in Large Language Models}\label{sec:hallucination}

A \emph{hallucination}\index{hallucination} is a generated statement that is
fluent and syntactically well formed, but that no source the model can point to
supports. Frequently it is simply false. Huang et al.~\cite{huang2025survey}
survey the phenomenon at length. They treat it as an intrinsic consequence of how
these models are built, not as an incidental defect to be patched away. That
framing matters here. If hallucination were a bug, the response would be to fix
it. Because it is structural, the response must be architectural.

\subsection{Where Hallucination Originates: Training Data, Model, and Prompt}

It is useful to separate three loci at which a hallucination can originate,
because they admit very different interventions.

The first is the \emph{training data}. A model trained on a corpus containing
errors, staleness, or under-representation of a domain will reproduce those
defects. Correcting them means curating the corpus and retraining, which is
outside the reach of anyone who is not the model's producer.

The second is the \emph{model} itself --- its parameterisation and decoding
procedure. Knowledge is stored diffusely across weights with no explicit index,
so the model has no reliable internal signal distinguishing a fact it has
memorised from a plausible continuation it is constructing. Interventions here
mean changing the architecture or the training objective, again largely out of
reach.

The third is the \emph{prompt}. This is the locus the thesis addresses. If the
context supplied alongside the question contains the facts required to answer it,
the model need not fall back on parametric recall. Structured context can
override defects inherited from training data without touching the model. That is
the premise behind retrieval augmentation~\cite{lewis2020retrieval}, and behind
the present work. The contribution lies in \emph{how} that context is assembled,
and in \emph{what is checked} before the answer is emitted.

\subsection{Why Multi-Hop Questions Are the Hard Case}

Single-fact questions are comparatively benign. ``Where was Ada Lovelace born?''
requires one lookup, and either the model knows it or it does not.

\emph{Multi-hop}\index{multi-hop question} questions --- ``Who directed the film
that won Best Picture in 1998?'' --- require composing an ordered chain of
lookups, each conditioned on the previous result. Two properties make these the
hard case. First, errors compound. A wrong resolution at the first hop is not
usually detected downstream, so the remainder of the chain proceeds confidently
from a false premise. Second, the intermediate results are exactly what a
question-level correctness check cannot see. A system that answers correctly by
accident and a system that answers correctly by sound reasoning are
indistinguishable from the final string alone.

Multi-hop questions also carry \emph{constraints} that must be satisfied jointly
rather than sequentially, whether temporal, categorical, or ordinal. Dropping one
silently is among the most common ways such questions are answered wrongly rather
than not at all.

\section{From Retrieval Augmentation to Graph-Structured
  Grounding}\label{sec:rag-to-graph}

\subsection{Vector Retrieval and Its Structural Blind Spot}

Retrieval-Augmented Generation~\cite{lewis2020retrieval} attaches the model to an
external corpus. The question is embedded, semantically similar passages are
retrieved by vector similarity, and the model answers from them. Where the answer
resides in a single retrievable passage, this works well.

Its weakness is structural. Vector retrieval treats a corpus as flat chunks of
text and ranks them by similarity to the question. For a multi-hop question that
signal is misleading. The passage holding the \emph{intermediate} entity may bear
little lexical or semantic resemblance to the original question, and the system
does not yet know which entity to look for. Relationships between entities are
not represented at all. They are at best implicit in adjacency within a retrieved
chunk, and they are precisely what a compositional question traverses. Surveys of
the intersection between graphs and language models identify this as the central
limitation of flat retrieval~\cite{li2023survey}.

\subsection{Knowledge Graphs and Static GraphRAG}

A \emph{knowledge graph}\index{knowledge graph} makes relationships explicit.
Facts are stored as typed edges between identified entities, so composing facts
becomes traversal rather than inference over prose. GraphRAG~\cite{edge2024local}
exploits this by building a graph over a corpus and retrieving structured context
rather than passages. Related systems extend the idea to long-term
memory~\cite{gutierrez2024hipporag} and to temporally-qualified
facts~\cite{li2025tgrag}.

These systems nonetheless retrieve \emph{statically}. A subgraph around the
linked entities is fetched in one shot and handed to the model. The retrieval
step does not know what the reasoning step will need, because it happens first
and only once. Consider a question whose second hop depends on the answer to its
first. A fixed-radius neighbourhood is either too small to contain the answer, or
so large that the relevant edges are buried among thousands of irrelevant ones.

\subsection{Agentic Retrieval: Reasoning as Navigation}

The natural response is to interleave retrieval with reasoning. The model takes
one step, observes what it found, and decides where to look next. Retrieval
becomes \emph{navigation}. The system becomes an agent operating on the graph
rather than a pipeline reading from it.

Think-on-Graph~\cite{sun2024think} established the pattern with LLM-guided beam
search over the graph. Plan-on-Graph~\cite{chen2024plan} added sub-objective
decomposition and reflection-driven backtracking, and
Reasoning-on-Graphs~\cite{luo2024reasoning} generates grounded relation paths as
explicit plans. Further systems add tool abstractions~\cite{jiang2024kgagent},
handle incomplete graphs~\cite{xu2024generate}, or generalise across structured
sources~\cite{jiang2023structgpt}. Recent surveys treat this convergence of
language models and knowledge graphs as a distinct research
programme~\cite{pan2024unifying,ma2025unifying}.

Agentic navigation is the right shape for the problem, and this thesis adopts it.
The question is what remains unsolved once it is adopted.

\section{Limitations of Existing Agentic KGQA Systems}\label{sec:limitations}

\subsection{No Check on What the Final Answer Asserts}

Agentic systems ground their answers in a traversed subgraph, and this thesis's
own measurements confirm that they do so effectively. The entities they assert
exist in the graph, and are reachable along the paths they walked
(\Cref{sec:groundedness-results}). The deficit is not fabrication.

The deficit is \emph{precision}. Every system surveyed in \Cref{ch:related} emits
whatever its final generation call produces from the traversed context. Nothing
checks that each asserted entity actually answers the question that was asked. An
entity can be present in the retrieved subgraph, correctly retrieved, and still
be the wrong answer. It may be an intermediate node from the reasoning chain, a
container where a member was requested, or one of several plausible neighbours
selected arbitrarily. It may also be one of many entities asserted where the
question admits few, which inflates recall at the cost of precision. Both
failures are invisible to a system that verifies retrieval but never verifies
assertion. They surface as a measurable precision gap against a system that does
check (\Cref{sec:webqsp-results}).

Chain-of-Verification~\cite{dhuliawala2024chain} and multi-agent
debate~\cite{du2024improving} establish that post-hoc self-checking reduces
factual error in free-form generation. A self-correcting agentic graph pipeline
has been demonstrated in a clinical setting~\cite{hu2025selfcorrecting}. What is
missing is a claim-level check against the retrieved triples, performed
\emph{before} the answer is emitted, inside a graph-navigating agent.

\subsection{Unquantified Contribution of Individual Agentic Mechanisms}

Agentic systems are assemblies of mechanisms: decomposition, scoring,
backtracking, self-correction. They are reported as wholes. When such a system
outperforms a static baseline, the literature rarely establishes which mechanism
produced the gain, or whether some contribute nothing at all. Without that
attribution, subsequent work inherits the entire assembly on faith, and its cost
along with it. Component-level ablation is the standard remedy. It is
conspicuously underused in this literature.

\subsection{Accuracy Reported Without Cost}

Navigation is expensive. Each exploration step costs at least one model call, and
agentic systems routinely consume an order of magnitude more tokens per question
than a single-shot baseline. Accuracy tables that omit this make agentic methods
look uniformly preferable, when the honest picture is a trade-off. That trade-off
is exactly what a practitioner deciding whether to deploy such a system needs to
see.

\section{Problem Statement}\label{sec:problem}

This thesis addresses the following problem. Given a natural-language question
$q$ and a knowledge graph $G = (E, R, T)$ with entities $E$, relation types $R$,
and triples $T \subseteq E \times R \times E$, produce an answer set
$A \subseteq E$ together with a supporting triple set $S \subseteq T$, such that
every entity in $A$ is justified by $S$, and $S$ consists only of triples the
system actually traversed in reaching $A$.

Three properties distinguish this from standard knowledge graph question
answering. First, the output is a \emph{pair}, not an answer alone. The
supporting triples are part of the contract, which makes the reasoning auditable
rather than asserted. Second, the support must be \emph{traversed}, not merely
retrievable after the fact. That forecloses post-hoc justification of an answer
arrived at some other way. Third, the justification is checked \emph{before} the
answer is emitted. Unsupported content can then be removed or hedged rather than
merely flagged.

\section{Research Questions}\label{sec:rqs}

\subsection{RQ1: Does Agentic Navigation Improve Multi-Hop Factual Accuracy?}

Does interleaving retrieval with reasoning over a knowledge graph produce more
accurate answers than static retrieval, and does the advantage grow with the
number of hops a question requires? Answering this needs a no-retrieval control,
since the standard benchmarks predate current models and may be partly
memorised.

\subsection{RQ2: What Does Pre-Generation Verification Contribute Beyond Graph
  Navigation?}

Given a system that already navigates the graph, what does a claim-level check
against traversed triples add? The question is posed as \emph{what does it
contribute}, not \emph{does it reduce hallucination}, because the answer turns
out to have several parts that a yes-or-no framing would obscure
(\Cref{sec:findings}).

\subsection{RQ3: Which Components Contribute What, at What Token Cost?}

Decomposition, backtracking, verification, and learned candidate scoring each
cost model calls. Which of them measurably improve accuracy or groundedness, by
how much, and at what price in tokens and latency?

\section{Our Contribution}\label{sec:contribution}

\subsection{The AGR Framework and Its Verification Layer}

We present Agentic Graph Reasoning (AGR), a knowledge-graph question-answering
framework built as an explicit state machine over a constrained, deterministic
graph tool API. It carries a \emph{Structural Verification Layer} that decomposes
the draft answer into atomic claims and checks each against the traversed
subgraph, before the answer is emitted. Unsupported claims trigger targeted
re-exploration, or removal from the answer. The system returns the answer paired
with its supporting triples.

\subsection{A Component-Level Ablation of Agentic Mechanisms}

We ablate four components: the planner, the backtracking mechanism, the
verification layer, and the learned component of candidate scoring. For each
condition we report accuracy, groundedness, and token cost, with paired
significance testing. This addresses the attribution gap identified in
\Cref{sec:limitations}.

\subsection{Stratum-Dependent Decomposition: When Planning Hurts}

Question decomposition is treated in the literature as straightforwardly
beneficial. We find it is not: removing the planner \emph{improves} accuracy on
the shallower benchmark while trending toward harming it on the deeper one. The
asymmetry, not the pooled average, is the result.

\subsection{The Echo Attractor as a Named Failure Mode}

We identify and characterise a recurring failure in which multiple independent
systems converge on the same wrong answer. It is typically the question's own
topic entity, an intermediate node from the reasoning chain, or a
coarser-granularity container. Because it is shared across systems, it is
invisible to any evaluation that treats systems as independent.

\subsection{Quantified Benchmark Defect Rates for WebQSP and CWQ}

We quantify label defects in the two standard benchmarks through a consensus
pre-pass followed by per-item adjudication. Two kinds of label error are
separated, because they have opposite evidence signatures. We report results both
with and without the affected items.

\subsection{An Evaluation Protocol with Pre-Registered Thresholds}

Four decisions were fixed before test data was touched: the scoring
hyperparameters, the sample size, the baseline-certification diagnostic, and the
judge-agreement bar. We report outcomes against all four, including the one that
was narrowly missed.

\section{Scope and Delimitations}\label{sec:scope}

The knowledge environment is a static, curated subset of
Freebase~\cite{bollacker2008freebase}. It is specifically the union of
per-question subgraphs that were pre-extracted so as to contain their own
question's answer, which makes it a friendlier place to search than the full
knowledge base and puts answer reachability above $97\%$ by construction
(\Cref{sec:validation-gate}). Absolute accuracies reported here are therefore not
comparable to evaluations over full Freebase; every system compared navigates the
identical environment, so the comparisons are, and \Cref{sec:threats} states the
limit in full. The thesis does not address graph
construction from unstructured text, knowledge-base completion, or the temporal
evolution of facts. Questions are English and factoid, drawn from
WebQSP~\cite{yih2016value} and ComplexWebQuestions~\cite{talmor2018web}. Long-form
generation and conversational settings are out of scope.

All systems share one backbone model, held constant so that architectural
differences are not confounded with model capability. The thesis therefore
reports how these architectures compare on a single backbone, not how they scale
across backbones. Finally, the system is a research prototype evaluated offline.
Latency is reported as a cost measure, not as evidence of production readiness.

\section{Thesis Organization}\label{sec:organization}

\Cref{ch:prelim} establishes the background required for the technical chapters.
It covers knowledge graphs and their Freebase-specific idiosyncrasies, knowledge
graph question answering and its evaluation conventions, language models as
reasoning engines, retrieval augmentation, graph search, and the statistical
tools used throughout. \Cref{ch:related} surveys prior work and positions AGR
against it. It culminates in a comparison of agentic knowledge graph question
answering systems, which identifies the gap this thesis addresses and justifies
the choice of baselines.

\Cref{ch:environment} describes the construction and validation of the knowledge
environment, including the answer-reachability gate that bounds the accuracy
attainable in it. \Cref{ch:framework} specifies the AGR framework: the tool API,
the state machine, the planner, the scoring function, the backtracking policy,
and the budgets that guarantee termination. \Cref{ch:verification} treats the
Structural Verification Layer in detail, as the thesis's principal architectural
contribution.

\Cref{ch:setup} sets out the experimental design: test sets, baselines, metrics,
judge validation, gold-answer quality control, and the pre-registration policy.
\Cref{ch:results} reports results and answers the three research questions.
\Cref{ch:erroranalysis} analyses the failures that remain, including the
benchmark defects and the shared-attractor pattern, and discusses threats to
validity. \Cref{ch:conclusion} summarises the contributions, states the
limitations, and identifies future work.
