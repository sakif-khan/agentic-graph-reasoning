\chapter{Introduction}\label{ch:intro}

Large language models answer factual questions fluently, and often enough they
get them wrong. The errors are not random; they come out systematic and
confident, and they are notoriously hard to catch from the output alone, because
a fabricated answer reads in exactly the same register as a correct one. They
also get worse, and quickly, once an answer calls for composing several facts
rather than recalling a single one. This thesis takes on that gap in two steps.
First, we give the model a symbolic knowledge graph to walk through. Then,
before it asserts anything, we check what it is about to say.

The rest of this chapter motivates the problem and explains why the obvious
remedies still fall short. From there it states the problem
and the research questions, summarises our contributions, and sets out how the
rest of the document is organised.

\section{Hallucination in Large Language Models}\label{sec:hallucination}

A \emph{hallucination}\index{hallucination} is a generated statement that is
fluent and syntactically well formed but backed by no source the model can point
to; very often it is simply false. Huang et al.~\cite{huang2025survey} survey the
phenomenon at length and argue that it is an intrinsic consequence of how these
models are built, not an incidental defect to be patched away. That framing
matters here, because it determines what kind of remedy is on the table at all.
If hallucination were a bug, we would fix it. Because it is structural, the
response has to be architectural.

\subsection{Where Hallucination Originates: Training Data, Model, and Prompt}

Hallucinations arise at three distinct points, and it is worth separating them
because each calls for a very different kind of intervention.

One is the \emph{training data}. A model trained on a corpus that carries
errors, staleness, or thin coverage of a domain will reproduce those defects
faithfully. Fixing them means curating the corpus and retraining, something only
the model's producer can do.

A second is the \emph{model} itself: its parameterisation and its decoding
procedure. What a model knows is stored diffusely across its weights, with no
explicit index, so it holds no reliable internal signal that separates a fact it
has memorised from a plausible continuation it is simply constructing.
Intervening here means changing the architecture or the training objective,
which is again largely out of reach.

The third is the \emph{prompt}, and this is the one this thesis takes on. When
the context supplied alongside a question already carries the facts needed to
answer it, the model does not have to fall back on parametric recall, and
structured context can override defects inherited from the training data without
touching the model at all. Retrieval augmentation~\cite{lewis2020retrieval} rests
on that premise, and so does this work. Our contribution is in \emph{how} that
context is assembled, and in \emph{what gets checked} before the answer goes out.

\subsection{Why Multi-Hop Questions Are the Hard Case}

Single-fact questions are comparatively benign. ``Where was Ada Lovelace born?''
takes one lookup, and the model either knows it or it does not.

\emph{Multi-hop}\index{multi-hop question} questions, such as ``Who directed the
film that won Best Picture in 1998?'', call for an ordered chain of lookups in
which each step depends on the result of the last. Two things make them the
genuinely hard case. The first is that errors compound: a wrong resolution at the
first hop rarely surfaces downstream, so the rest of the chain proceeds
confidently from a false premise. The second is that a question-level
correctness check never sees the intermediate results at all, which leaves a
system that answers correctly by accident indistinguishable from one that
answers correctly by sound reasoning.

These questions also tend to carry temporal, categorical, or ordinal
\emph{constraints} that have to be satisfied together rather than one at a time.
Dropping one silently is one of the commonest ways a system gets such a question
wrong instead of simply declining to answer it.

\section{From Retrieval Augmentation to Graph-Structured
  Grounding}\label{sec:rag-to-graph}

\subsection{Vector Retrieval and Its Structural Blind Spot}

Retrieval-Augmented Generation~\cite{lewis2020retrieval} attaches the model to an
external corpus. The system embeds the question, retrieves passages that are
semantically similar to it, and lets the model answer from those. Where the
answer lives in a single retrievable passage, this works well.

Its weakness, though, is structural. Vector retrieval treats a corpus as flat
chunks of text and ranks them by similarity to the question, and for a
multi-hop question that signal is actively misleading. The passage holding the
\emph{intermediate} entity may bear little lexical or semantic resemblance to the
original question, and at that point the system does not yet know which entity to
look for. Flat retrieval also has no way to represent the relationships between
entities; they survive, at best, as adjacency inside a retrieved chunk, and
relationships are exactly what a compositional question has to traverse. Surveys
of the intersection between graphs and language models single this out as the
central limitation of flat retrieval~\cite{li2023survey}.

\subsection{Knowledge Graphs and Static GraphRAG}

A \emph{knowledge graph}\index{knowledge graph} makes those relationships\index{GraphRAG}
explicit. It stores facts as typed edges between identified entities, so
composing two facts becomes a traversal rather than an inference over prose.
GraphRAG~\cite{edge2024local} exploits exactly this, building a graph over a
corpus and retrieving structured context in place of raw passages, and related
systems carry the idea into long-term memory~\cite{gutierrez2024hipporag} and
temporally-qualified facts~\cite{li2025tgrag}.

What these systems still do is retrieve \emph{statically}: they fetch a subgraph
around the linked entities in one shot and hand it to the model. Because
retrieval runs first and runs only once, it cannot know what the next reasoning
step will need. Take a question whose second hop depends on the answer to its
first. A fixed-radius neighbourhood is then either too small to contain that
answer, or so large that the relevant edges are buried among thousands of
irrelevant ones.

\subsection{Agentic Retrieval: Reasoning as Navigation}

The natural response is to interleave retrieval with reasoning: let the model
take one step, look at what it found, and decide where to look next. Retrieval
then turns into \emph{navigation}, and the system becomes an agent operating on
the graph rather than a pipeline reading from it.

Think-on-Graph~\cite{sun2024think} set the pattern with LLM-guided beam search
over the graph. Plan-on-Graph~\cite{chen2024plan} added sub-objective
decomposition and reflection-driven backtracking, and
Reasoning-on-Graphs~\cite{luo2024reasoning} generates grounded relation paths as
explicit plans. Other systems add tool abstractions~\cite{jiang2024kgagent},
cope with incomplete graphs~\cite{xu2024generate}, or generalise across
structured sources~\cite{jiang2023structgpt}. Recent surveys treat this meeting
of language models and knowledge graphs as a research programme in its own
right~\cite{pan2024unifying,ma2025unifying}.

Agentic navigation is the right shape for the problem, and this thesis adopts it.
What remains open is what it still leaves unsolved.

\section{Limitations of Existing Agentic KGQA Systems}\label{sec:limitations}

\subsection{No Check on What the Final Answer Asserts}

Agentic systems ground their answers in the subgraph they traverse, and our own
measurements confirm that they do this effectively: the entities they assert
really do exist in the graph, and they are reachable along the paths the systems
walked (\Cref{sec:groundedness-results}). Fabrication is not the deficit here.

The deficit is \emph{precision}. Every system we survey in \Cref{ch:related}
emits whatever its final generation call produces from the traversed context,
and nothing checks that each asserted entity actually answers the question that
was asked. An entity can sit in the retrieved subgraph, have been retrieved
correctly, and still be the wrong answer, whether it is an intermediate node
from the reasoning chain, a container where the question asked for a member, or
one of several plausible neighbours picked more or less at random. A system can
equally well assert a crowd of entities where the question admits only a few,
which inflates recall at the cost of precision. A system that verifies retrieval
but never verifies assertion cannot catch either failure, and both show up as a
measurable precision gap between systems (\Cref{sec:webqsp-results}). That gap is
what motivates a check in the first place. It is not, on its own, evidence that
the check works, and \Cref{sec:ablations} is where this thesis asks whether the
check proposed here is actually what produces it.

Chain-of-Verification~\cite{dhuliawala2024chain} and multi-agent
debate~\cite{du2024improving} show that post-hoc self-checking reduces factual
error in free-form generation, and Hu et al.~\cite{hu2025selfcorrecting}
demonstrate a self-correcting agentic graph pipeline in a clinical setting. What
none of them provides is a claim-level check against the retrieved triples,
carried out \emph{before} the answer is emitted, inside a graph-navigating agent.

\subsection{Unquantified Contribution of Individual Agentic Mechanisms}

Agentic systems are assemblies of mechanisms, things like decomposition, scoring,
backtracking, and self-correction, and the literature tends to report them as
wholes. When one of these systems beats a static baseline, the published work
rarely establishes which mechanism actually produced the gain, or whether some of
them contribute nothing at all. Without that attribution, later work inherits
the entire assembly on faith, along with its cost. Component-level ablation is
the standard remedy, and this literature conspicuously underuses it.

\subsection{Accuracy Reported Without Cost}

Navigation is expensive. Every exploration step costs at least one model call,
and agentic systems routinely burn an order of magnitude more tokens per
question than a single-shot baseline. An accuracy table that leaves this out
makes agentic methods look uniformly preferable, when the honest picture is a
trade-off, and that trade-off is exactly what a practitioner needs to see before
deciding whether to deploy such a system.

\section{Problem Statement}\label{sec:problem}

We address the following problem. Given a natural-language question $q$ and a
knowledge graph $G = (E, R, T)$ with entities $E$, relation types $R$, and
triples $T \subseteq E \times R \times E$, produce an answer set $A \subseteq E$
together with a supporting triple set $S \subseteq T$, such that $S$ justifies
every entity in $A$ and contains only triples the system actually traversed in
reaching $A$.

Three properties set this apart from standard knowledge graph question
answering. The output is a \emph{pair}, not an answer on its own: the supporting
triples are part of the contract, which makes the reasoning auditable rather than
merely asserted. That support also has to be \emph{traversed}, not just
retrievable after the fact, which closes off the possibility of justifying, post
hoc, an answer the system reached some other way. And the system checks the
justification \emph{before} it emits the answer, so it can drop or hedge
unsupported content instead of merely flagging it.

\section{Research Questions}\label{sec:rqs}

\subsection{RQ1: Does Agentic Navigation Improve Multi-Hop Factual Accuracy?}

Does interleaving retrieval with reasoning over a knowledge graph actually yield
more accurate answers than static retrieval, and does the advantage grow with the
number of hops a question requires? Answering this calls for a no-retrieval
control, because the standard benchmarks predate current models and may be partly
memorised.

\subsection{RQ2: What Does Pre-Generation Verification Contribute Beyond Graph
  Navigation?}

Given a system that already navigates the graph, what does a claim-level check
against the traversed triples add? We pose this as \emph{what does it contribute}
rather than \emph{does it reduce hallucination}, because the answer turns out to
have several parts that a yes-or-no framing would only obscure
(\Cref{sec:findings}).

\subsection{RQ3: Which Components Contribute What, at What Token Cost?}

Decomposition, backtracking, verification, and learned candidate scoring each
cost model calls. Which of them measurably improve accuracy or groundedness, by
how much, and at what price in tokens and latency?

\section{Our Contribution}\label{sec:contribution}

\subsection{The AGR Framework and Its Verification Layer}

We present Agentic Graph Reasoning (AGR), a knowledge-graph question-answering
framework built as an explicit state machine over a constrained, deterministic
graph tool API. It carries a \emph{Structural Verification Layer} that decomposes
the draft answer into atomic claims and checks each one against the traversed
subgraph before the system emits anything. Where a claim is unsupported, the
design either routes to targeted re-exploration or drops the claim from the
answer, and AGR returns what survives paired with the triples that support it.

Both repair routes are specified, implemented, and reported at the frequency we
actually measured rather than the one we intended. That measured frequency is
low: the re-exploration route never fired on the development set, because the
depth budget is already exhausted by the time the verifier is reached, and the
entity filter ran on only three of eighty questions (\Cref{ch:verification}). On the
vast majority of questions the layer simply passes through and certifies a draft
at no extra model call. That is a finding about when verification can act, not a
defect in it, and \Cref{sec:findings} treats it as one.

\subsection{A Component-Level Ablation of Agentic Mechanisms}

We ablate four components, namely the planner, the backtracking mechanism, the
verification layer, and the learned component of candidate scoring, and report
accuracy, groundedness, and token cost for each condition, complete with paired
significance testing. For one architecture, at least, this closes the attribution
gap we identify in \Cref{sec:limitations}.

\subsection{Stratum-Dependent Decomposition: When Planning Hurts}

The literature treats question decomposition as straightforwardly beneficial. We
find that it is not. Removing the planner \emph{improves} accuracy on the
shallower benchmark while trending toward harming it on the deeper one, and the
asymmetry, not the pooled average, is the real result. Only the improving arm
reaches significance ($p = 0.006$); the harming arm does not ($p = 0.088$), and
we report it as a direction rather than as an effect.

\subsection{The Echo Attractor as a Named Failure Mode}

We identify and characterise a recurring failure in which several independent\index{echo attractor}
systems converge on the same wrong answer, typically the question's own topic
entity, an intermediate node from the reasoning chain, or a coarser-granularity
container. Because the systems share it, no evaluation that treats them as
independent can even see it. It accounts for $13$ of the $259$ failures read in
\Cref{sec:echo}, which is where the evidence for it lives; the contribution is
the named mechanism itself and what it means for consensus-based evaluation, not
the frequency.

\subsection{Quantified Benchmark Defect Rates for WebQSP and CWQ}

We quantify label defects in the two standard benchmarks through a consensus\index{benchmark defect}
pre-pass followed by per-item adjudication. We separate two kinds of label error
because they carry opposite evidence signatures, and we report results both with
and without the affected items.

\subsection{An Evaluation Protocol with Pre-Registered Thresholds}

We fixed four decisions before touching any test data: the scoring
hyperparameters, the sample size, the baseline-certification diagnostic, and the
judge-agreement bar. We report how each of the four turned out, including the one
we narrowly missed.

\section{Scope and Delimitations}\label{sec:scope}

Our knowledge environment is a static, curated subset of
Freebase~\cite{bollacker2008freebase}, specifically the union of per-question
subgraphs, each pre-extracted so as to contain its own question's answer. That
makes it a friendlier place to search than the full knowledge base, and it puts
answer reachability above $97\%$ by construction (\Cref{sec:validation-gate}).
The absolute accuracies we report are therefore not comparable to evaluations
over full Freebase, though the comparisons between systems are, because every
system navigates the very same environment; \Cref{sec:threats} states the limit
in full. We do not take on graph construction from unstructured text,
knowledge-base completion, or the temporal evolution of facts, and we draw only
English factoid questions, from WebQSP~\cite{yih2016value} and
ComplexWebQuestions~\cite{talmor2018web}. Long-form generation and
conversational settings are outside our scope.

All systems share one backbone model, which we hold constant so that none of the
differences we report can be credited to model capability rather than to
architecture. What we establish, then, is how these architectures compare on a
single backbone, not how they scale across backbones. Finally, AGR is a research
prototype that we evaluate offline, and we treat latency as a cost measure
rather than as evidence of production readiness.

\textbf{How to read the title.} This thesis is submitted under the title
registered with CASR at the proposal stage, which is fixed at registration and is
not revised here. Three of its terms are broader than what the chapters below
actually establish, and they are better read against the findings than taken as
claims in their own right. \emph{Hallucination mitigation} does occur, but it is
not this work's contribution: \Cref{sec:groundedness-results} attributes the
elimination of structural hallucination to graph navigation, with the agentic
baseline reaching the same zero without any verification layer at all.
\emph{Autonomous} names a design this thesis argues against; under the control
pattern of \Cref{sec:architecture} the program orchestrates the loop and the
model is consulted only at constrained decision points. And \emph{fact
verification} is narrower here than the phrase suggests, since the check
establishes structural adjacency rather than the relation a claim asserts, and
never reaches answer relevance (\Cref{sec:verify-failure-modes}). Put in the
terms the measurements actually support, what follows is a claim-level structural
check placed before emission, together with a component-level attribution of the
mechanisms around it.

\section{Thesis Organization}\label{sec:organization}

\Cref{ch:prelim} establishes the background the technical chapters assume. It
covers knowledge graphs and their Freebase-specific idiosyncrasies, knowledge
graph question answering and its evaluation conventions, language models as
reasoning engines, retrieval augmentation, graph search, and the statistical
tools we use throughout. \Cref{ch:related} surveys prior work and positions AGR
against it, ending with a comparison of agentic knowledge graph question
answering systems that pinpoints the gap this thesis addresses and justifies our
choice of baselines.

\Cref{ch:environment} describes how we build and validate the knowledge
environment, including the answer-reachability gate that bounds the accuracy
attainable inside it. \Cref{ch:framework} specifies the AGR framework: the tool
API, the state machine, the planner, the scoring function, the backtracking
policy, and the budgets that guarantee termination. \Cref{ch:verification}
treats the Structural Verification Layer in detail, which is the component this
work set out to contribute and, as it happens, the one whose measured effect
turns out to be the narrowest of the three reported in \Cref{sec:findings}.

\Cref{ch:setup} lays out the experimental design: test sets, baselines, metrics,
judge validation, gold-answer quality control, and the pre-registration policy.
\Cref{ch:results} reports what we measured and answers the three research
questions. \Cref{ch:erroranalysis} works through the failures that remain,
including the benchmark defects and the shared-attractor pattern, and discusses
threats to validity. \Cref{ch:conclusion} summarises the contributions, states
the limitations, and points to future work.
