Two annotation tasks in this thesis produced hand labels: the gold-quality
adjudication of \Cref{sec:gold-quality}, and the failure census of
\Cref{ch:erroranalysis}. This appendix gives the instructions and the label

\section{Judge Instructions}\label{app:judge-rules}

The semantic-support judge of \Cref{sec:judge} decides one question per claim:
is this claim supported by these triples? Eight rules constrain that judgement.
Each was forced by a disagreement encountered while validating the judge against
a human annotator, and the validation outcome --- $\kappa = 0.6995$ against a
pre-registered bar of $0.70$ --- is reported in \Cref{sec:judge} rather than
here.

\begin{enumerate}
\item Judge the question that was asked, not the question the claim would have
  answered well. A true statement about the topic entity is unsupported if the
  question asked about something else.
\item Support must come from the triples shown. A claim that is true in the
  world but absent from the triples is unsupported.
\item A claim inferable from the triples only by adding outside knowledge is
  unsupported. This is the rule that separates the judge from the model's own
  parametric memory, and it is the one that most often decided a disagreement.
\item Surface form is not the test. Where the graph names an entity
  \textsf{University Yale} and the claim says \textsf{Yale University}, the
  claim is not unsupported for that reason alone.
\item Direction is not the test either. A triple asserted in one direction
  supports a claim stated in the other, unless the relation's meaning is
  genuinely asymmetric.
\item A composite claim is supported only if every component is supported.
  Partial support is unsupported.
\item A hedge asserts nothing and therefore has no claims to judge. It is
  neither supported nor unsupported.
\item Where the triples are ambiguous between two readings and one supports the
  claim, judge it supported. The metric is intended to detect assertion without
  grounding, not to penalise underspecified evidence.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Failure Census Label Schema}\label{app:census-schema}

Every packet in the census receives one \textsf{category}, an optional
\textsf{subtype}, and a one-sentence note. The note pinpoints where the
trajectory \emph{first} left the correct path --- plan, then explored relations,
then backtracks, then verifier --- rather than the last symptom. That ordering
is the whole discipline of the exercise: a wrong answer usually has several
things wrong with it by the end, and only the first one is a cause.

\subsection*{Convention for the verifier categories}

The verifier's positive class is \emph{claim is supported}. A false negative is
therefore a claim wrongly \emph{rejected}, and a false positive is a claim
wrongly \emph{accepted}. This is the standard convention and the one the census
labels use throughout. It is stated explicitly because the gloss in the working
reference originally paired the two names in reverse, and two Stage~A labels
were filed against the reversed reading before the error was found. Both were
corrected; \Cref{sec:census-corrections} records them individually.

\section{Label Provenance and Regeneration}\label{sec:census-corrections}

The census is hand labour recorded in files that scripts also write, so which
file is authoritative for which field is not obvious from inspection.
\Cref{tab:labelfiles} settles it.

\begin{table}[!htb]
  \begin{center}
    \caption{Where each label lives, and what regenerating will do to it.}
    \label{tab:labelfiles}
    \footnotesize
    \begin{tabular}{|l|L{88mm}|}
      \hline
      \textbf{File} & \textbf{Status} \\
      \hline
      \textsf{labels\_\{ds\}.csv} &
      The hand-labelled census. \textsf{dump\_failure\_packets.py} rewrites the
      file but carries existing labels forward by question ID, so hand edits
      survive a re-run. \\
      \hline
      \textsf{noplanner\_categories\_\{ds\}.csv} &
      Generated, with no carry-forward. Labels belong in
      \textsf{noplanner\_discordant\_\{ds\}.md}, which
      \textsf{pull\_noplanner\_categories.py} transcribes into this CSV. An edit
      made directly to the CSV is overwritten on the next run. \\
      \hline
      \textsf{labels\_\{ds\}\_dropped.csv} &
      An input to the histogram rather than an archive:
      \textsf{synthesize\_census.py} merges its rows into the totals. Nothing
      regenerates it, because it is written only at the moment a row leaves the
      population. If it is lost, recover it from version control. \\
      \hline
      \textsf{sampling\_manifest.json} &
      Written but never read back. Its carried, to-read and dropped fields are
      progress counters. The thesis cites only the population and skip counts,
      which are stable across re-runs. \\
      \hline
    \end{tabular}
  \end{center}
\end{table}

After any relabelling, three scripts must run in order:
\textsf{pull\_noplanner\_categories.py}, then \textsf{synthesize\_census.py},
then \textsf{build\_thesis\_numbers.py}. Skipping the last leaves the thesis
quoting the previous histogram.

\subsection*{Corrections made after the first labelling pass}

Three changes were made to labels after the initial pass, and are recorded here
rather than silently absorbed.

\begin{itemize}
\item The false-positive and false-negative gloss in the working reference
  paired the two names in reverse of the standard convention. It was corrected,
  and the convention is now stated explicitly above. The census labels
  themselves had followed the standard convention throughout and were not
  changed.
\item \textsf{WebQTrn-1294} moved from \textsf{verifier\_fn} to
  \textsf{answer\_selection}. The verifier had been correct; the error was
  upstream, in the answerer.
\item \textsf{WebQTrn-1597} moved from \textsf{verifier\_fn} to
  \textsf{verifier\_fp}, the original label having used the reversed
  convention.
\end{itemize}

The histogram was regenerated afterwards. The totals are unchanged; three CWQ
hedge rows moved between categories.
